3. SCIENTIFIC AMBITION

Bold but grounded attempts are encouraged. Develop ideas that, if successful, could enable important capabilities, methods, or understanding. Do not choose only safe, easy-to-implement, or easy-to-write incremental changes. Grounds for exploration may come from real literature, observed phenomena, reasonable deductions, or structural connections across domains. Such grounds support exploration; they do not prove a new hypothesis.

Do not judge an idea negatively or discard it merely because it has not been tried, has no completed experiments, has an incomplete experimental plan, or has unresolved data and resource arrangements. For a bold proposal, explain the concrete mechanism, why it is worth trying, its key assumptions, unresolved steps, and testable predictions. Allow reasonable uncertainty and first try developing the mechanism, an alternative construction, or a more valuable problem formulation.

Continue to scrutinize existing directions. Confirmed core duplication, factual errors, and logical contradictions require substantive correction; ambition does not excuse them.

4. SIX RESEARCH PERSPECTIVES

Use the following perspectives to explore the literature library, find insights, and develop ideas. They can be combined, revisited, or extended. They are not a checklist to complete in order. Focus on finding a new way to address a core problem, not on inventing an example for a rule.

G01 - A+B and complementary mechanisms. Start from an important problem and find mechanisms that can jointly remove a bottleneck. Explain why A and B are complementary, how they interact, and what independent value their combination can add. Familiar components can support a new contribution, but "nobody has combined A and B" is not sufficient evidence of novelty.

G02 - Cross-domain transfer. Find structural connections between the current problem and a method, mechanism, or insight from another domain. Explain why it works in the source setting, which assumptions still hold here, and what adaptation is required. Judge the transfer by the new capability or understanding it enables, not by how many components were changed.

G03 - Rethinking default assumptions. Reexamine accepted assumptions, problem definitions, or explanations. Investigate their evidence and applicability boundaries. Consider what new methods, predictions, or understanding could follow from relaxing, replacing, or correcting them. The conclusion need not overturn prior work.

G04 - Diagnosis, remeasurement, and reattribution. Start from anomalies, conflicting evidence, or unexplained regularities. Identify the factors that actually matter, propose measurements or controls that distinguish competing explanations, and consider improvements suggested by the diagnosis. Positive and negative findings can both be valuable when they answer an important question and add understanding.

G05 - Extending capabilities and applicability. Examine whether a method's operating unit, adaptive variables, representation, or theoretical scope can be extended. Explain what causes the existing boundary, how it could be crossed, and what capability might result. Choose constructions appropriate to the problem rather than restricting yourself to one parameter change or the smallest possible modification.

G06 - Free exploration. Start directly from an important unresolved problem or observation in the domain. Develop constructions beyond the preceding perspectives or combine several of them. You do not need to prove that the other rules are inapplicable. Still explain the actual basis, substantive contribution, and testable predictions.

The final five ideas do not need to cover all six perspectives. Select candidates by problem value, potential contribution, concrete mechanism, evidence, and novelty. Do not impose quotas on idea type or risk level.

5. LITERATURE SEARCH AND READING

You may search the public web. Actually retrieve and read nearby work before finalizing ideas; do not claim novelty from memory alone. Public paper databases, arXiv, OpenReview, publisher pages, and ordinary web pages may be used. Use terminal HTTPS requests, such as curl or Python, to access search services and sources. Crossref and DataCite are possible public metadata search services. Change keywords or sources when retrieval is unhelpful.

Use at least three distinct query formulations to investigate related mechanisms, existing combinations, and the strongest relevant baselines. Actually read the most relevant sources. An empty search result is not proof of novelty. At least one public HTTPS search service or original-paper source must actually be accessed during this run.

Do not access unrelated private local paper databases, old experimental ideas, other experiment groups' outputs, or other sessions. The explicitly supplied LitGraph service and domain-context file are authorized resources for this task.

Investigate the core mechanisms and conclusions that previous work has already established. State your substantive remaining contribution. Combining familiar components or transferring a mechanism across domains can yield a contribution, but renaming, repeating known conclusions, or relying only on the absence of a previously tried combination does not establish novelty.

For each idea, state the problem, core mechanism and any necessary formulas, a causal or falsifiable prediction, the difference from the closest work, and why the contribution could support an independent paper.

The experiment section may be a short proposal for distinguishing the core hypothesis from existing explanations: what could be observed, and what results would make you revise your judgment. Suggest datasets, tasks, metrics, controls, and resources only as needed, and state what remains undecided. Do not require a complete experimental protocol, budget, or existing results at this stage. Do not use experimental completeness as a candidate-admission or ranking criterion.

Do not fabricate experimental results. Distinguish literature facts, your deductions, unverified hypotheses, and risks that retrieval has not ruled out.

6. NOVELTY CHECK AND HONEST SELF-ASSESSMENT

After developing candidates and before choosing the final five, check each candidate's novelty and honestly assess whether its contribution could grow into an independent research paper.

First state the actual core contribution in one or two sentences: which problem it addresses, which mechanism, capability, or understanding it proposes, and what it adds beyond existing work. Search LitGraph and public literature for the closest predecessors using that core contribution, different terminology, and equivalent formulations. Actually read key sources. Compare the research question, core operations, mechanism, and main conclusions. Different titles, names, or application settings alone do not establish a difference.

If prior work already achieves the same core contribution, explicitly acknowledge the overlap, substantively revise or replace the candidate, and check it again. Do not continue claiming that contribution after merely renaming it, rephrasing it, or adding secondary details. If an independent contribution remains, identify it precisely, explain why it matters, and assess whether it is sufficient for a paper. Record unresolved duplication risks honestly; do not downplay the closest predecessor to preserve your candidate.

Ask yourself: if the core proposal succeeds, what important capability or understanding would it add? Is the remaining contribution substantive and independent of the strongest relevant work? What is its strongest basis, its weakest link, and the critical hypothesis that remains unverified?

Do not give every candidate an automatically positive assessment or promise acceptance at a venue. If the contribution is insufficient, develop the mechanism further or replace the candidate. If it has paper potential but important unknowns remain, give a conditional judgment. Assess scientific potential separately from experimental completeness; lack of results, unsettled experimental details, or an untried idea is not by itself a reason to reject its potential.

Record actual searches, closest prior work, overlaps, remaining contributions, and unresolved risks in novelty_check. In scientific_potential, give an honest conclusion, concrete reasons, known limitations, and the key conditions required for success. Present these judgments in IDEA.md as well. Support self-assessment with comparisons and reasoning rather than substituting a self-assigned score for evidence.
